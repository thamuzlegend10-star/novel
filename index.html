Blind Hope
Ch. 1 — Nicole Ch. 2 — Cici Ch. 3 — Kellen

Chapter One

The Weight I Built in the Dark

A story about a rejection that didn't break anything — it just asked to be carried differently.

There are people who enter your life the way weather enters a season — not announced, not chosen, simply arrived — and by the time you notice them, they have already rearranged something in you. Nicole was weather like that. A slow front moving in before I knew to look at the sky.

I. Before the Silence

I don't remember the exact day I met her. That's the strange thing about elementary school — nothing announces itself as important while it's happening. Grade four, maybe. We were never close. Just normal friends, the kind you sit near but don't think much about — except for one thing I'm not proud of.

I was careless back then, the way kids are careless before they understand that consequences land on people and stay there. One recess, we were playing a cheap, improvised version of soccer — the kind with no real rules, just a plastic water bottle standing in for a ball. I kicked it without thinking, the way you kick something a hundred times without a second thought, except this time it caught her in the head. It was an accident. I knew that even then. But I remember her crying, and I remember standing there afterward not knowing what to do about it — not knowing how to fix something I hadn't meant to break in the first place. That helplessness stuck with me longer than I expected it to.

But there was another side to it, one I didn't have words for yet. Nicole was the first person who ever made me look at someone differently — not as a classmate, not as a name on a seating chart, but as someone I noticed. A small, quiet kind of attraction I didn't understand and definitely didn't act on. I never made a move. I don't think I even let myself call it anything at the time. It just sat there, unspoken, the first flicker of something I wouldn't recognize for what it was until years later.

Then elementary school ended, the way it does for everyone — abruptly, without ceremony — and we drifted. Different schools, different lives. Years passed. I didn't think about her every day. But she didn't disappear completely either. She just went quiet, the way old songs go quiet in your head until something plays them again.

II. The Message

Grade nine. I don't know what made me do it — maybe boredom, maybe nostalgia, maybe some part of me that wanted to know if the person I remembered was still in there. I opened Instagram and typed out a message to Nicole.

She replied.

We talked. Old memories resurfaced, the kind that feel sweeter with distance than they probably were in the moment. And somewhere in those conversations, something in me shifted — the quiet elementary-school friendship started to feel like something else. Something I wanted to say out loud.

So I did something objectively ridiculous, and completely sincere: I wrote her a poem.

It wasn't good. It was the kind of poem that starts with the oldest line in the world — roses are red, violets are blue — except I twisted it, because I wanted it to be mine. I replaced the flowers with something heavier, something that would last: minerals. Gold. Iron. I don't remember the exact lines anymore, but I remember the feeling behind them — I was trying to say you are something rare, and I am something that doesn't bend easily, and I have been carrying this for longer than you know.

I sent it.

III. The Reply

She wrote back quickly. Kindly. Devastatingly.

"Thank you for confessing, but we have already lost contact for a few years, so I think we should just be friends."

That was it. No cruelty in it. No mockery. If anything, it was the gentlest possible way to say no — and somehow that made it worse, the way a wound cut by a careful hand still bleeds. There was nothing to be angry at. She hadn't done anything wrong. I couldn't even be bitter about it. I had handed her my heart wrapped in a silly poem about gold and iron, and she had handed it back with more grace than I gave myself credit for receiving — the way you might return something beautiful that was never yours to keep, careful not to let it break on the way.

I didn't reply with anything dramatic. I think I just said something small, something that let both of us pretend the moment wasn't as big as it felt. Then I put my phone down and became, for the first time, truly and privately alone with it.

IV. What I Didn't Tell Anyone

Here's what nobody around me knew: I was fine. I went to school. I talked to my friends. I laughed when things were funny. Nothing on the surface cracked.

But underneath, for about a month, I was drowning quietly. Nobody noticed — not my closest friends, not my family. No one asked, because there was nothing on the outside to ask about. I decided, somewhere without consciously deciding, that this was mine to carry alone. It would take years before I told anyone at all — not until senior high school, when it had long since stopped being a wound and had become just a story I could finally say out loud.

So I carried it at midnight.

V. The Ritual

My room became the only place the truth was allowed to exist. It wasn't much to look at — a hideous little space whose only window faced a blank wall, some dead air shaft in the middle of the house that let in no view, just a thin wash of light from outside at night, barely enough to see by. Alone, door closed, the rest of the house asleep, I started doing something I'd never really done before: I worked out.

Push-ups. Sit-ups. Skipping rope, over and over, the rhythm of it filling the silence where a reply from her might have been. Shadow boxing at things that weren't there — throwing punches at a version of the night that couldn't hit back, could only absorb whatever I threw at it and ask for more.

It wasn't about getting stronger. Not at first. It was about doing something with a feeling that had nowhere else to go, a grief with no funeral, no name I was willing to give it. I wasn't chasing a body. I was chasing exhaustion — the kind that lets you fall asleep instead of think, the way a river wears a stone smooth not through violence but through repetition, night after night, until something that used to cut you stops cutting.

But bodies remember what you do to them at midnight, even if your heart doesn't notice. Months passed the way seasons do, imperceptibly and then all at once. What started as skinny arms and a frustrated kid throwing punches at nothing slowly became something else. Strength that hadn't been there before. Muscle where there used to just be bone and restlessness. I was rebuilding myself in the dark, brick by invisible brick, and I didn't even realize that's what it was until other people started to notice the difference in daylight — as if I had been quarried out of my own sadness.

VI. The Slow Return

About three months after the rejection, Nicole and I started talking again — carefully, at first, the way you test ice before trusting it to hold your weight. It never went back to exactly what it was. How could it? But it became something real in its own way: an honest friendship, built on the fact that I had asked for more and she had told me the truth instead of stringing me along.

I kept working out. Grade nine turned into grade ten, grade ten into eleven. The ritual outlived the pain that started it — push-ups and shadow boxing became less about her and more about who I was becoming. But I never really stopped because of that first wound. Even as the ache faded, the discipline stayed, like a habit that had learned to survive without its original reason.

VII. The Angel

Somewhere around grade ten, something happened that still confuses me when I try to make sense of Nicole as a character in my own life: she started helping me. Actively. She guided me toward someone else — someone the relationship with whom would go on to turn out well — even though I never worked up the courage to confess to that person either, some old trauma quietly holding my tongue.

That's the part of Nicole I still don't fully know how to describe. She was the one who rejected me. And she was also the one who, without needing to, chose to help me find something good afterward. She never owed me that. She did it anyway.

She wasn't a villain in this story. She wasn't a prize I lost, either. She was something stranger and more human than both of those things — a person who told me the truth when it would've been easier to let me hope, and who then spent the following years quietly rooting for me to be happy with someone else.

She blessed me. And she cursed my feelings. Both of those things are true, and neither one cancels the other out.

VIII. What Was Left When the Pain Faded

By grade eleven, the nightly ritual started to fade — not because the pain came roaring back to be defeated once and for all, but because it simply healed, the way most real wounds do: slowly, unevenly, without a single dramatic final scene.

And somewhere in that healing, someone new started to matter.

But that story — the one Nicole helped set in motion without either of us fully realizing it — belongs to the next chapter.

What Nicole left behind wasn't heartbreak. It was a body that could carry more than it used to, a friendship that survived its own worst moment, and a strange, permanent gratitude for a girl who said no in the kindest way a no has ever been said — the way certain doors, closed gently, teach you more about love than the ones left open ever could. She spent years afterward making sure that no wasn't the end of the story, only the sentence that taught me how to keep writing — the beginning of a harder, better one.

End of Chapter One.

Chapter Two

The Hint I Never Caught

Some chapters are about what happened. This one is about what almost did — and never got the chance to, because I wasn't looking.

I. The Friend Who Liked to Play

Cici Aulia had been around since grade four, the same stretch of years that gave me Nicole, except Cici was a different kind of presence entirely — loud where Nicole was quiet, mischievous where Nicole was steady. She was the kind of friend who liked to tease, to push buttons just to watch your reaction, a little naughty in the harmless, grade-school way kids are naughty when they haven't learned subtlety yet. I liked her the way you like a friend who makes boring days less boring. Nothing more than that, as far as I knew.

II. What I Didn't See

Grade five. Somewhere in there, without my noticing a single sign of it, Cici developed a crush on me.

I want to say I was oblivious in some charming, forgivable way — but the truth is I was just young and self-absorbed in the specific way boys that age are, too busy existing inside my own head to notice when someone was quietly existing around the edges of it, hoping to be noticed. Whatever signs there were, I missed every one of them.

It took someone else to tell me. A break-time, school, nothing special about the day — and then my friend Kevin said it out of nowhere, the way guy friends do, like he was handing over a piece of gossip rather than a piece of information that might have mattered. She likes you. Did you not know?

I didn't.

III. Nothing Happened

And that was it — nothing happened. No confession from her, no confession from me. I don't know if the moment simply passed because neither of us reached for it, or if by the time I knew, whatever she'd felt had already quietly folded itself away. We stayed what we'd always been: classmates, friends, nothing declared. Life moved on the way it does for kids who don't yet understand that some windows only open once.

IV. Years Later, A Scroll

Senior high school. I was bored one evening, doing the thing everyone does when there's nothing else to do — scrolling back through old names on Instagram, half out of nostalgia, half out of nothing at all. I found her account.

Cici had changed. The naughty, playful grade-schooler I remembered had grown into someone who did modeling and cosplay, photos styled and confident in a way that felt like looking at a stranger who happened to share a childhood with me. And there, somewhere in the photos, was the detail that actually stopped my scrolling: she had a boyfriend.

V. The Regret That Didn't Stay

Something in me ached at that — not heartbreak, nothing that dramatic, but a quieter kind of pain, the specific sting of realizing a door you never knew was open had already closed somewhere behind you. For a while I sat with it. What if I'd noticed back then? What if Kevin's out-of-nowhere comment had landed on someone less oblivious?

But the ache didn't stay long, and I think that's the real shape of this chapter — not loss, but a small, honest regret that taught me something instead of haunting me. I had something to chase in the present. Someone else, something else, a version of myself still being built in rooms at midnight. I let Cici's chapter close the way it had always been quietly closing, one missed hint at a time, and I moved on.

Not every story needs an ending where you get the girl. Some stories just need you to notice the next time — and I think that's exactly what this one gave me.

End of Chapter Two.

Chapter Three

The Hours I Hoped Would Never Come

Some moments you spend your whole life hoping never to meet — and then one day, without warning, they knock anyway, and you let them in.

Cici's chapter was still closing quietly behind me when junior high opened its own door — slower this time, and far more complicated than anything that came before it.

Her name was Kellen. We'd been friends since grade seven, though for a long while that friendship barely registered as anything — just two names in the same crowd of names. I don't even remember the exact point where talking to her stopped being incidental and started being something I looked forward to. It just happened the way most real things happen: without an announcement.

By then I was still carrying what Nicole had left behind — the ache mostly quiet now, but not gone, tucked somewhere under two years of midnight push-ups and shadow boxing. I noticed Kellen anyway. The old wound didn't stop the new feeling from showing up; it just made me more careful about what I did with it.

The Switch I Almost Refused

Our school ran something called the SMP Sport and Art Festival every year — categories for everything, sport and performance both. I was already committed to Wushu, the discipline I'd trained in for a while by then, strong and straight-lined, built on stances and strikes rather than anything that moved like water. I also had basketball lined up.

Then a friend of mine, a boy in the Modern Dance category, asked if I'd switch with him — take his spot in dance, let him take mine in basketball.

My first instinct was no. Dance wasn't my category, wasn't my language. But Kellen was in that group. And somewhere between the asking and the answering, I did the math on what I actually wanted more — and said yes.

Learning a Flexibility I Didn't Have

Wushu had taught me one kind of flexibility — the kind built through stretching, through discipline, through forcing a joint slightly further than it wants to go. Modern dance wanted something else entirely. Not stretch, but sway. Not strike, but drift. My body, trained for years to be sharp and controlled, now had to learn how to be loose, almost liquid, in ways that felt embarrassing every single time I got it wrong — which, at first, was often.

I trained anyway. Awkward, stiff, clearly out of my depth next to people who moved like the choreography was already in their bones — I kept showing up, because showing up meant being near her.

Mobile Legends and a Story About Jeafer

Somewhere between rehearsals, during a shopping trip for performance outfits, Kellen and I exchanged Mobile Legends accounts — the kind of small, almost accidental intimacy that ends up mattering more than it should. She turned out to be good. I wasn't. We played constantly in the weeks leading up to the festival, voice chats bleeding into late evenings, the game becoming less about the game and more about an excuse to keep talking.

It was during one of those sessions, or maybe during a break at rehearsal, that she told the group about her ex — a guy named Jeafer. She said he used to like her, that they'd been something once, and that it ended because of how he treated her — some phrase along the lines of he just didn't treat girls right, I didn't like his attitude. We listened. I filed it away as one more piece of who she was.

The Performance

The final day arrived and I was nervous in a way Wushu competitions had never made me — Wushu had certainty, stances I'd drilled a thousand times. This had none of that. My movements were still stiff next to the others, still carrying the straight lines of a different discipline in a dance that wanted curves. But we performed anyway, the whole group moving as one, and somehow — stiffness and all — we placed top four.

Afterward, the whole group went to the mall together to celebrate, loud and relieved, everyone still half-dressed in performance clothes, laughing about nothing in particular. It felt, for a few hours, like something uncomplicated.

The Tease That Confirmed It

That night, back home, Kellen and I were chatting as usual when a DM came in from another friend — a girl who told me, flatly, that Kellen had a crush on me.

I did the only thing a part of me apparently knew how to do with information like that: I teased her about it, dropping hints, circling the subject until she realized I already knew. She didn't deny it. From there, something between us shifted into something clearer, unspoken but understood. Long school holidays arrived right after, and for a while it was just the two of us playing games for hours at a time, the kind of closeness that doesn't need a label to feel real.

The Salt in the Scar

Then holidays ended, and so did the uncomplicated part.

A few days after we were back at school, I saw Kellen with Jeafer — talking easily, comfortably, like old friends who had never had a bad word between them. It didn't match anything she'd told us. The same guy she said mistreated her, the same guy whose attitude she claimed to dislike, and here she was laughing next to him like none of that had ever been said.

It confused me before it hurt me. Then it did both. It wasn't even about losing her to him — it was the unnecessary nature of the lie, a story told for no clear reason that I could find, which made it worse somehow than if she'd simply changed her mind about him in silence.

We grew apart after that, slowly and then completely. She kept drifting back toward Jeafer. I kept drifting away from both of them.

What It Cost

This one didn't just add a new wound — it reopened the old one, Nicole's ache and this one fusing into something heavier than either alone. It lasted around five months, and the old ritual came back with it, harder this time, eating into sleep I didn't have to spare.

What it left behind wasn't discipline this time, just exhaustion — breaktimes and empty lesson periods turning into stolen twenty-minute naps, head down on a desk, in a classroom that had no idea what it was looking at. Nobody at school ever asked why I was always so tired. I don't think I would have known how to answer if they had.
